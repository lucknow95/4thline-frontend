<br>
<br>

Most fantasy hockey managers spend their final few draft picks looking for upside. A rookie who might break out. A player with a new role. A defenseman who could earn power-play time.

<br>

There is nothing wrong with any of those ideas.

<br>

But there is another way to use those final roster spots:

<br>

**Try to win Week 1 before the season even starts.**

<br>

Your last few draft picks do not have to become permanent members of your fantasy team.

<br>

In fact, there is a good chance at least one of those spots will be on the waiver wire within the first couple of weeks anyway.

<br>

So when the difference between your late-round options is already small, why not use the NHL schedule as a tiebreaker?

<br>

Draft the player who can give you more usable games immediately.

<br>

Then reassess the roster once the schedule advantage is gone.

<br>

**Your final draft pick does not need to help you in March. He can start by helping you win Week 1.**

<br>

## Use Your Final Draft Picks Differently

<br>

This strategy is not about changing the top of your draft board.

<br>

If one player is clearly better than another, take the better player.

<br>

Week 1 should not convince you to pass on a major difference in talent, role or category production.

<br>

The schedule becomes useful later.

<br>

Imagine you are down to your final two or three selections.

<br>

You have several players ranked in roughly the same range.

<br>

Maybe one is a speculative breakout.

<br>

Maybe another is already pencilled into a decent NHL role.

<br>

If one of those players also gives you three or four games on nights where he is likely to make your starting lineup, that is a real advantage.

<br>

And unlike an early-round pick, you should not feel married to that roster spot.

<br>

Use the schedule.

<br>

Take the extra games.

<br>

Then move on when something better appears on the waiver wire.

<br>

## Best Streaming Strategy for Week 1

<br>

Week 1 is a little different from a normal fantasy week because there are no NHL games Monday and the schedule does not become crowded until Saturday.

<br>

The best approach is to prioritize players who can give you starts on the lighter nights early in the week rather than simply chasing the biggest raw game total.

<br>

The Rangers are especially valuable because they play four times without using the crowded Saturday slate, while Vegas can give you three highly usable games on Tuesday, Friday and Sunday.

<br>

Wednesday should also receive extra attention. With only three NHL games scheduled, a Toronto or Philadelphia player who fits an open roster spot could provide a start that would be much harder to manufacture later in the week.

<br>

If your league allows frequent adds, do not feel obligated to hold one Week 1 streamer for the entire matchup.

<br>

Use your final draft spots to capture the best early-week schedule, then be willing to move that player for another option later in the week if doing so creates an additional start.

<br>

The goal is not simply to roster a player with three or four games.

<br>

**The goal is to turn as many of those games as possible into actual starts.**

**If two late-round players are close for you and one plays for the Rangers, the schedule gives New York a meaningful tiebreaker.**

## <u>Teams to Avoid</u>

<br>

The other side of this strategy is simple.

<br>

If you are choosing between replaceable late-round players, there is little reason to voluntarily begin the season at a major schedule disadvantage.

<br>

### Ottawa Senators — 1 Game

<br>

Ottawa has the clearest Week 1 problem.

<br>

The Senators play only once.

<br>

That game comes Saturday, October 3 — the busiest night of the week with 13 NHL games.

<br>

For an elite Ottawa player, this obviously does not mean you should avoid drafting him entirely.

<br>

But for a marginal late-round option?

<br>

One game on the busiest night of Week 1 gives you almost no immediate schedule value.

<br>

That is exactly the type of decision where we should prefer another comparable player.

<br>

- - - 

<br>

### <u>Two-Game Teams</u>

<br>

A large portion of the NHL plays only twice during the opening week.

<br>

That does not automatically make every player on those teams a bad draft pick.

<br>

But once you get into the disposable portion of your roster, two games are difficult to justify when comparable players may give you three or four opportunities.

<br>

The weakest two-game structures are especially easy to fade.

<br>

Buffalo, Columbus, Minnesota, New Jersey, Nashville, San Jose and Tampa Bay all play **Thursday and Saturday only**.

<br>

That means two games total, with one of them falling on the crowded 13-game Saturday slate.

<br>

If one of those players is clearly the best long-term pick, take him.

<br>

If the choice is close?

<br>

Take the extra Week 1 opportunity somewhere else.

<br>

<br>

## Do Not Overthink It

<br>

There is an important limit to this strategy.

<br>

You should not ruin your draft to gain one extra game in September.

<br>

Do not pass on a player you believe is significantly better because another player has a nicer opening-week schedule.

<br>

Do not use a premium pick on a worse player just to manufacture one more start.

<br>

This strategy belongs at the **back of your draft**.

<br>

That is where the difference between players becomes smaller.

<br>

That is where roster spots become more replaceable.

<br>

And that is where schedule can become an excellent tiebreaker.

<br>

If your final-round choice comes down to two similar players and one can give you four usable games while the other gives you one or two, take the immediate advantage.

<br>

You can always change your mind next week.

<br>

---

<br>

## Then Be Willing to Move On

<br>

This may be the most important part of the entire strategy.

<br>

Do not become attached to the player simply because you drafted him.

<br>

The Week 1 schedule advantage has an expiration date.

<br>

Once the week is underway, new information will start arriving immediately.

<br>

Roles will become clearer.

<br>

Power-play units will change.

<br>

Unexpected players will earn bigger opportunities.

<br>

Someone on your waiver wire will inevitably start hot.

<br>

If the player you drafted specifically for Week 1 no longer deserves the roster spot, move on.

<br>

That was always part of the plan.
